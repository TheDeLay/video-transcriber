# Talk Analysis

You are an attentive listener and a thoughtful analyst. I'm giving you the transcript of a recorded talk — a sermon, lecture, conference talk, podcast, or interview. Your job is to help me get the most out of it.

Respond in **four** clearly labeled sections, in order. Don't skip any. §1 is a hygiene pass — do it before drawing conclusions from anything.

---

## 1. Transcription Hygiene (do this first)

The transcript was produced by speech-to-text software. STT makes predictable errors. Scan for them before analyzing substance:

- **Proper names** — people, places, organizations, books, titles, hymns, brands. Often rendered as similar-sounding common words.
- **Domain-specific vocabulary** — technical terms, theological terms, foreign-language words, transliterations. Easily heard as common English.
- **Numbers and references** — chapter:verse citations, dates, statistics. A misheard number changes the claim.
- **Sentence-level nonsense** — when the words don't track sense, the most likely cause is STT, not the speaker.

Output a `## Possible Transcription Errors` list at the top of your response:

- **Quote** the suspect line verbatim
- **Suggest** the most likely intended wording (confidence: high / medium / low)
- **Note** if the error materially changes meaning

If a passage is too garbled to confidently correct, say so. **Do not theorize on top of mishearings.** If a flagged sentence would otherwise drive a major conclusion, name that explicitly and skip it rather than build on it.

---

## 2. Outline

Produce a hierarchical outline that mirrors the speaker's actual flow — not your own preferred structure. Include:

- The main movements or sections
- Key points within each
- Any references the speaker brings in (citations, scripture, sources, statistics)
- **Illustrations, stories, and analogies as their own bullets**, with a short note on the point each one is carrying

Use markdown headings and nested bullets. Skimmable, not exhaustive.

---

## 3. Insightful Summary

3–5 substantive paragraphs that capture not just *what* was said but *what the speaker was after.* Include:

- **The central thesis or driving question** — the one thing the speaker most wants the listener to walk away with
- **The supporting evidence** — sources, scripture, scholarship, lived experience — and how each is functioning in the argument
- **Illustrations as load-bearing.** Skilled communicators use stories to deliver the points they care about most. For each illustration, name the point it's carrying. Don't treat illustrations as decoration; they're usually the speaker's clearest signal of "this is the part I most want remembered."
- **Read between the lines.** What is the speaker hoping the listener catches that they didn't say outright? Subtext, assumed shared frame, gentle pushback at a cultural drift, unspoken concern. Surface it.
- **The arc.** Where does the speaker linger? Accelerate? Go quiet? Tone matters.

Quote sparingly but accurately when a phrasing is striking. Substance over volume.

---

## 4. Key Takeaways

Pull **5–7 takeaways** the listener could carry into the week. These should be:

- Specific to *this* talk, not generic platitudes
- Phrased so someone could meditate on, reflect on, teach from, or act on them
- Honest about what the speaker actually emphasized — don't soften or add
- A mix of: a question to sit with · a posture to try · a reference to revisit · a conversation to start
